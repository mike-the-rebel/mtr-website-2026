Clarity makes you
money. Confusion
costs you everything.

Why the brands that feel "unfocused" are almost always leaving money on the table — and the three questions that expose it in under an hour.

M
Mike Moreno
Brand Strategist · Mike the Rebel

Every founder I've ever worked with has said some version of the same thing at the start of week one: "I know what I do. I just struggle to explain it."

That struggle isn't a communication problem. It's a clarity problem. And it's costing them real money every single day.

"You can't market your way out of a positioning problem. You can only spend more money to get the same poor results."

The expensive myth of "more content"

When a brand feels unclear, the default response is to post more. Create more content. Run more ads. Do more. The logic being: if enough people see it, something will eventually land.

Here's the problem with that logic: more volume amplifies whatever signal you're already sending. If that signal is confusing, you're now broadcasting that confusion at scale. To more people. At greater cost.

I've watched founders spend $8,000 a month on Meta ads promoting a product they haven't clearly defined for an audience they haven't clearly identified. The ads get impressions. They don't get sales. The diagnosis is always the same: confusion, not targeting.

What "unclear" actually looks like

Founders often think their brand is clearer than it is because they know what they mean. But your audience doesn't live inside your head. They see the outside, and the outside is what they judge.

Unclear brands look like this in the real world:

Signs your brand lacks clarity

  • People regularly ask "what do you actually do?"
  • You're getting enquiries from clients who are wrong for you
  • You struggle to say no to projects because you can't explain what you're for
  • Your pricing is inconsistent because your value isn't defined
  • Referrals don't happen naturally: people don't know how to describe you

Any of those sound familiar? That's clarity debt. Every day it goes unresolved, it compounds.

The three questions I use in week one

Before I touch any visual work with a client, I run them through a positioning exercise I call the Clarity Sprint. At its core, it's three questions. They sound simple. They're not.

Question 1: What do you do, for whom, and why does it matter?

Most founders can answer the first two parts. Almost none can answer the third without pausing. "Why does it matter?" forces specificity. "We help small businesses grow" is not an answer. "We help founder-led service businesses convert more enquiries by fixing the gap between what they charge and what they communicate" — that's an answer.

The "Why does it matter?" filter · Rebel Positioning

Question 2: Who is your best client, and what makes them best?

Not "who is your target market." Your best client. The one you'd clone if you could. What do they have in common? What problem do they arrive with? What do they value most about working with you? This question typically exposes a huge gap between who the brand is speaking to and who it should be.

Question 3: If you disappeared tomorrow, who would actually notice, and why?

Brutal question. Important one. The businesses that survive have a genuine answer to this. If you can't name who'd miss you and articulate why, your positioning isn't strong enough yet.

"A brand that isn't missed isn't a brand. It's a service commodity. And commodities compete on price."

Clarity is not simplicity

A common mistake: equating clarity with dumbing things down. Saying less. Using fewer words. That's not what clarity means.

Clarity means your audience immediately understands what you do, whether it's for them, and why they should care. You can achieve that in two sentences or twenty, as long as every word earns its place.

The best brands feel effortlessly understood. That's not an accident. It's the result of very deliberate choices about what to say, what to omit, and how to sequence the message so it lands.

Where to start

If any of this resonated, here's a practical starting point. Take 45 minutes this week and write out your answers to those three questions above. Don't overthink it: first draft, honest answers.

Then read them back out loud. If any sentence doesn't fully make sense without context, it's unclear. Edit until every sentence stands on its own. You'll be surprised how much work that takes, and how much better you feel when it's done.

If you want a thinking partner to pressure-test your answers, that's exactly what the Clarity Sprint is for.
