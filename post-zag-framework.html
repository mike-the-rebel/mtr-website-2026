How to find your brand's angle when every competitor looks exactly the same. Four prompts I use with every founder in week one.

There's a game being played in almost every industry. The rules are unspoken but universally followed: study what the successful players in your space do, and do roughly the same thing. Same tone, same visual language, same service categories, same pricing structure.

The logic sounds reasonable. They're working, so it must be working.

But here's the problem: when everyone zigs, the most powerful move is to zag.

"Differentiation isn't found in being better at the thing everyone's doing. It's found in doing a different thing entirely."

ZAG.

The Zag Framework · Rebel System

Why "better" isn't enough

In a crowded market, claiming to be better is a losing strategy. "Better quality," "better service," "better value": every competitor says the same thing. None of it lands because none of it is distinctive.

Distinctiveness comes from difference, not improvement. When your brand occupies a specific angle that no-one else holds, it doesn't need to compete. It simply exists in its own space.

The Zag Framework is how I find that angle.

The four prompts

Prompt 1: What do the best brands in your category always do that you could consciously not do?

Start by mapping the conventions of your industry. What tone do they all use? What visual language? What do they lead with in their messaging? What's the implied promise?

Now ask: which of these conventions could you break without breaking trust? That gap is often where the angle lives.

Mapping industry conventions · Step one of the Zag process

Prompt 2: What does your best client complain about with everyone else in your space?

Your existing clients are a gold mine of positioning intelligence. The thing they were frustrated about before they found you: that's your angle. Name it explicitly. Build your positioning around the solution to their frustration.

If your clients consistently say "everyone else was so jargon-heavy / slow / transactional / impersonal," lean all the way into the opposite. Make it your signature.

Where to find your angle

  • Client onboarding notes: what problems did they arrive with?
  • Exit conversations: what did they say changed for them?
  • Competitor reviews: what do unhappy customers of rivals complain about?
  • Your own instincts: what do you do differently without thinking?

Prompt 3: What do you do as a matter of course that most in your field consider optional?

Every practitioner has non-negotiables that most of their peers treat as nice-to-haves. For some it's strategy before design. For others it's radical communication transparency, or deep research before advice, or refusing to take on misaligned clients.

Your non-negotiables are your standards. And standards, made visible, become brand.

"Your standards aren't constraints. They're your brand's most powerful differentiator, if you're willing to say them out loud."

Prompt 4: Who is your brand definitively not for, and why?

This is the one most founders resist. Exclusion feels like lost revenue. But a brand that tries to speak to everyone speaks to no-one.

Naming who you're not for is one of the most powerful positioning moves available. It signals to the right people that you're exactly for them. It signals to the wrong people to look elsewhere, which saves everyone time and protects the quality of your work.

Putting it together

Run these four prompts and you'll have the raw material for a genuine positioning angle. It won't be perfect on the first pass. Positioning is iterative. But you'll be working with real insight rather than aspirational platitudes.

The brands that make the biggest impact in their markets aren't the ones who did the most. They're the ones who made the most deliberate choices about who to be and who to be it for.

Zag deliberately. It's the only move that can't be copied.
